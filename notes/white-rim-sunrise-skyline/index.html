GIS Method | Canyon Country Sunrise

Reading First Light Off a DEM

A step-by-step ArcGIS Pro method for predicting when the sun clears a desert rim. The method gets checked against a known landmark before the sunrise number is trusted.

LocationWhite Rim Road, Canyonlands National Park, Utah
DataUSGS 3DEP, 10 m (33 ft) digital elevation model
ToolsArcGIS Pro 3D Analyst, Python
PublishedNovember 9, 2026

A digital elevation model, or DEM, is a raster surface of ground height. This note uses one to answer a question most sunrise apps do not answer: from a specific campsite on the White Rim Road in Canyonlands National Park, does the sun rise between two distant peaks, and when does direct light actually clear the near rim?

The question a flat horizon cannot answer

Most sunrise apps assume a flat horizon. In canyon country that assumption is wrong, because the real horizon is a nearby rim, not an open plain. A hand calculation against an assumed camp height put the near rim between two and four degrees above flat, with a flat-horizon sunrise guess around 6:50 a.m. Two different assumptions in that calculation, the camp position and the camp elevation, later turned out to be wrong. A terrain surface removes both guesses.

The data and the tool

The elevation data is a USGS 3DEP digital elevation model at 10-meter resolution, downloaded from The National Map and covering the horizon out past the distant peaks in question. The sunrise run used one tile, n39w110, in the release dated June 30, 2026. I downloaded it on September 4, 2026. The main tool is Skyline, in the 3D Analyst extension of ArcGIS Pro. Skyline reads a DEM and an observer point, then traces the terrain’s horizon angle in every compass direction, or azimuth, around that point. A second tool, Skyline Graph, turns that horizon line into a table of horizon angle by azimuth.

Method, in six steps

  1. Download the 10 m DEM and project it to a metric coordinate system. Skyline needs elevation and ground distance in the same units, and a DEM left in geographic degrees gives wrong angles with no warning. I used NAD83(2011) UTM zone 12N.
  2. Place an observer point at the campsite, using the position in the trip’s GPS log. Raise it 6.6 feet (2 m) above the surface. Without that offset, the point can read as sitting just below the terrain and gets silently dropped from the run.
  3. Run Skyline from that point across bearings 70° to 95° in 0.25° steps, out to 43 miles (70 km). Turn on earth curvature and atmospheric refraction. Over tens of miles, curvature alone moves a distant peak by hundreds of feet of apparent height.
  4. Run Skyline Graph against the resulting horizon line to produce a table of horizon angle by bearing.
  5. Match the bearings. Skyline measures bearings from the north of the map grid, not true north. At this camp the two differ by 0.64°. Add 0.64° to a Skyline bearing to get a true bearing.
  6. Trace the sun. Compute the sun’s altitude and true bearing every second from 6:30 a.m. Correct for refraction and add the sun’s half-width, so the test uses the sun’s upper edge. The first second when that edge is above the horizon angle at the sun’s own bearing is first direct light.

Step 6 replaces a shortcut. The shortcut reads the horizon angle at one sunrise bearing and divides it by how fast the sun climbs, about 11° an hour. The sun does not climb straight up. Between sunrise and first light here, it moves about four degrees of bearing along a rim that falls by about one degree.

The sun positions come from the NOAA solar-position equations. I checked them against a second method, the Astronomical Almanac low-precision formulas. The two agree within 0.01° of altitude and bearing, which is about two seconds of time.

Checking the method before trusting the answer

A terrain method is only as good as its inputs, so the run gets checked against a landmark with a known position before the sunrise number is trusted. Candlestick Tower is a named rock spire about 1.9 miles (3 km) from the campsite, with a documented position. Predicted by hand, the tower sits at a true bearing of 76.03° and rises 8.84° above level. Skyline measured the same tower at 75.75° from grid north, which is 76.39° true, and at 8.62° above level.

That is agreement to about a third of a degree of bearing and a fifth of a degree of height. At 1.9 miles, 0.36° is about 62 feet (19 m). That is within the limits of the landmark’s published position and Skyline’s 0.25° step. The agreement shows the observer point, the map projection, the elevation values, and the curvature settings are sound. The pattern is reusable at any new site: pick one obstruction whose position you already know, predict its angle, and confirm the run reproduces it before reading the number the trip needs.

An earlier run used an observer point about 70 feet (21 m) south of the campsite. I moved the point to the logged position and ran Skyline again. First light moved by 7 seconds, from 7:19:07 to 7:19:14 a.m. The check above uses the second run.

The result

From the campsite, the sun’s upper edge clears the rim at about 7:19 a.m. At that moment the sun stands at a true bearing of about 85°, where the rim is 4.6° above level. Flat-horizon sunrise at the same spot is about 6:54 a.m. So the rim delays first direct light by about 26 minutes. In that time the sun moves about four degrees of bearing along a rim that falls from about 5.5° to 4.6°.

Across the sun’s path, the near rim stands about 2.9° to 3.7° above the distant La Sal terrain behind it. The sun does not rise between the La Sal peaks from this camp. The near rim is in the way first.

Skyline did not produce the dashed line in the chart below. I computed it from the same DEM. For each 0.25° of bearing, it is the highest angle among cells 25 to 43 miles (40 to 70 km) away, with the same curvature and refraction settings.

The shortcut from the method section gets within a few minutes here. Its error depends on small choices, such as the bearing convention and the starting time. The trace avoids those choices.

What the morning actually did

The terrain answer is not the same as the photograph. Monsoon cloud moved in overnight. At 6:54 a.m., about when the flat-horizon sun rose, glow from the sky lit the clouds above the eastern horizon. Candlestick Tower stands in silhouette, and the ground in front of it is in shade. No direct sun has reached the camp, as the model predicts. Usable light lasted about three minutes, then cloud cover took over. The 7:19 a.m. moment never happened in clear sky. The terrain calculation holds up. The weather decided what the camera recorded.

What this note cannot show

This note rests on a terrain model, one morning, and one set of photographs. It has two limits:

  • No photograph tests the 7:19 a.m. time. Cloud covered the sky, and no frame looks at the rim at that moment.
  • The photographs only weakly test the claim that the rim hides the La Sal peaks. In the frames from 6:53 to 6:55 a.m., no distant peak rises above the near rim. That fits the model. Cloud and glow limit what the frames can show, so the elevation model carries this claim.

Elevation data from the USGS 3D Elevation Program, distributed through The National Map. Horizon angles computed with the Skyline and Skyline Graph tools in ArcGIS Pro 3D Analyst. Distant terrain angles computed from the same DEM. Sun positions from the NOAA solar-position equations.
