GIS Method — Canyon Country Sunrise

Reading First Light Off a DEM

A step-by-step ArcGIS Pro method for predicting true sunrise behind a desert rim. The method gets checked against a known landmark before the sunrise number is trusted.

LocationWhite Rim Road, Canyonlands National Park, Utah
DataUSGS 3DEP, 10 m digital elevation model
ToolsArcGIS Pro, 3D Analyst

A digital elevation model, or DEM, is a raster surface of ground height. This note uses one to answer a question a phone app cannot: from a specific campsite on the White Rim Road in Canyonlands National Park, does the sun rise between two distant peaks, and when does direct light actually clear the near rim?

The question a flat horizon cannot answer

Every sunrise app assumes a flat horizon. In canyon country that assumption is wrong by ten to forty minutes, because the real horizon is a nearby rim, not an open plain. A hand calculation against an assumed camp height put the near rim between two and four degrees above flat, with a first-light guess around 6:50 a.m. Two different assumptions in that calculation, the camp position and the camp elevation, later turned out to be wrong. A terrain surface removes both guesses.

The data and the tool

The elevation data is a USGS 3DEP digital elevation model at 10-meter resolution, downloaded from The National Map and covering the horizon out past the distant peaks in question. The main tool is Skyline, in the 3D Analyst extension of ArcGIS Pro. Skyline reads a DEM and an observer point, then traces the terrain’s horizon angle in every compass direction, or azimuth, around that point. A second tool, Skyline Graph, turns that horizon line into a table of horizon angle by azimuth.

Method, in five steps

  1. Download the 10 m DEM and project it to a metric coordinate system. Skyline needs elevation and ground distance in the same units, and a DEM left in geographic degrees gives wrong angles with no warning.
  2. Place an observer point at the campsite, raised a small height above the surface. Without that offset, the point can read as sitting just below the terrain and gets silently dropped from the run.
  3. Run Skyline from that point, aimed at the sunrise sector, with earth curvature and atmospheric refraction turned on. Over tens of kilometers, curvature alone moves a distant peak by hundreds of meters of apparent height.
  4. Run Skyline Graph against the resulting horizon line to produce a table of horizon angle by azimuth.
  5. Read the horizon angle at the sunrise azimuth, 82.6° for this site and date, and convert it to a time delay using how fast the sun climbs near the horizon at this latitude, about 11° an hour.

Checking the method before trusting the answer

A terrain method is only as good as its inputs, so the run gets checked against a landmark with a known position before the sunrise number is trusted. Candlestick Tower is a named rock spire near the campsite with a documented position. Predicted by hand, the tower sits at azimuth 76.03° and rises 8.84° above flat. Skyline measured the same tower at azimuth 75.50° and 8.62°.

That is agreement to about half a degree of bearing and a fifth of a degree of height. It shows the observer point, the map projection, the elevation values, and the curvature settings are all sound, agreement that close is not likely by chance. The general pattern is reusable at any new site: pick one obstruction whose position is already known, predict its angle, and confirm the run reproduces it before reading the number the trip actually needs.

The result

At the sunrise azimuth of 82.6°, Skyline measured a horizon angle of 4.99°. Converted to time, that puts first direct light around 7:18 a.m., about 28 minutes after the flat-horizon sunrise. Across the sunrise sector the near rim stands roughly 2.6° to 3.4° above both distant peaks behind it. The sun does not appear to rise between the two peaks from this camp; the near rim is in the way first.

What the morning actually did

The terrain answer is not the same as the photograph. On the morning in question, monsoon cloud moved in overnight. Usable light lasted about three minutes, starting near 6:55 a.m., then cloud cover took over. The predicted 7:18 a.m. moment never happened in clear sky. The terrain calculation held up; the weather decided what the camera actually recorded.

What is not verified yet

This page stays a draft because three items are still open. Stating them here rather than leaving them out:

  • The DEM tile identifiers for this Skyline run are not recorded. The tool ran against a downloaded 10 m tile, but the exact tile name and download date for this specific run were not written down at the time.
  • The observer latitude used in the Skyline run disagrees with the latitude in the trip’s GPS log by about 21 meters. At the rim distances involved, that gap barely moves the horizon angle, but the two records still disagree, and neither has been checked against a coordinate measured standing at the site.
  • The claim that the near rim hides the distant peaks has not been checked against the camera frames. Any frame shot between 6:20 and 7:00 a.m. that shows the eastern skyline should show the rim standing above both peaks. That check has not been run yet.

Next: a second site

A trip to Island Park, Idaho, from September 19 to 21, 2026, is planned as a second worked example of the same method. Running the same five steps at a different site, with a different rim and a different sunrise bearing, is the real test of whether this method generalizes past one campsite.

Draft note. Elevation data from the USGS 3D Elevation Program, distributed through The National Map. Horizon angles computed with the Skyline and Skyline Graph tools in ArcGIS Pro 3D Analyst.
