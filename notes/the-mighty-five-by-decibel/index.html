The Mighty Five, by Decibel

Across 17% of Utah, people add at least 6 decibels of background sound in the National Park Service (NPS) model. The five national parks range from 0% to 11%.

Sound added by people

The National Park Service (NPS) modeled sound twice: under existing conditions and with human inputs minimized. This map shows the difference.

The metric is A-weighted L50, the sound level exceeded half the time. A-weighting reflects how human hearing responds to different frequencies. The predictions describe a typical summer daytime hour in calm weather.

Decibels (dB) use a logarithmic scale. NPS relates a 3 dB rise in background sound to half the listening area. At 10 dB, one tenth remains. At 6 dB, about 25.1% remains, rounded to a quarter on the map. This explains the key; the mapped quantity stays in decibels.

Count on the source grid

day11_sound.py checks that the existing and natural rasters share a grid. It subtracts natural from existing sound on the original 270 m cells.

Cells count when their centers fall inside the Utah Geospatial Resource Center (UGRC) state boundary or an NPS park boundary. Utah contains 3,016,210 cells, including lakes. The script found no invalid cells inside the state. The display uses nearest-neighbor resampling into a Utah-centered Albers equal-area projection. Display pixels do not determine the figures.

Why the medians stay off the meters

NPS reports predicted levels within 3.1 dB at half of natural sites. Errors are larger at other sites. That figure describes level predictions, not a measured error bound for their difference.

The parks’ median increments range from 1.70 to 3.02 dB. Those small differences cannot support a confident park ranking. The 6 dB threshold is farther from that error scale, but it is not a significance test.

Bryce Canyon has only 1,996 cells, the fewest of the five parks. Its boundary estimate is coarse. These figures describe modeled areas, not individual listening locations.

  • The model describes expected conditions, not measurements made for this map.
  • Sound levels are often lower at night and in winter.
  • The analysis does not identify a cause for any individual sound band.

Day 11 bonus

Within a Mile of a Tornado Siren

For Day 11, Brooks Groves mapped the tsunami sirens of Washington. He drew each siren with the one-mile circle that it is designed to be heard in. This bonus asks the same question about tornado sirens in Oklahoma. Oklahoma City and Tulsa publish where their outdoor warning sirens stand, and tornadoes have crossed both cities many times.

74% of Oklahoma City is within 1 mile (1.6 km) of one of its 182 sirens. 81% of the city of Tulsa is within 1 mile of one of its 97 sirens. These figures are shares of the land inside the city limits. They are not shares of people.

The siren data

Oklahoma does not publish one statewide layer of siren locations. Two metro areas publish their own, so the poster maps those two areas.

  • Oklahoma City publishes 182 sirens in a city feature service. The layer has no date and no metadata. Its count matches the 182 sirens that the city gives on its website.
  • The Tulsa Area Emergency Management Agency (TAEMA) file has 244 sirens in Tulsa and nine nearby towns. 97 of the sirens belong to the City of Tulsa. The file is dated April 10, 2012. The City of Tulsa serves it in its hazard mitigation map service.
  • The TAEMA file gives a range for each siren, in feet. I checked that unit against the agency’s own range circles, and they agree to within 0.1%. Most ranges are 6,000 or 6,100 feet (about 1.1 miles, or 1.8 km).
  • Moore, Edmond and other cities near Oklahoma City have their own sirens. I did not find their locations as open data, so the Oklahoma City map shows only the city’s sirens.

Distance, not sound

Sirens are built to warn people outdoors. Wind, terrain, buildings and background noise change how far a siren carries. The Washington map uses a published one-mile design range. Oklahoma City publishes no range, so this poster does not use the TAEMA ranges either. It makes no claim about what people can hear. The rings mark distance from the nearest siren every quarter mile (0.4 km), and the bright edge is 1 mile.

The city limits come from the Census Bureau’s 2024 TIGER/Line places. For each city, the script makes a circle around each of the city’s own sirens, joins the circles, and divides the covered area by the area of the city.

Tornado paths

The NOAA Storm Prediction Center (SPC) publishes each recorded tornado path since 1950 as GIS data. From 1950 to 2024, 142 Oklahoma tornado records cross or touch the Oklahoma City limits. 34 records cross or touch the Tulsa limits. No record repeats. Some older records have only a touchdown point and no end point. Those records still count.

SPC draws each path as a straight line from start to end. The map widens each line to its recorded width in yards and softens the edges. Where paths overlap, the streaks get brighter. Ratings use the F scale before 2007 and the EF scale after, as SPC records them.

Coverage on top

The first drafts drew the tornado paths as solid gray bands. The paths drew the eye before the sirens. That is the wrong order for a map about sound. In the final poster, the coverage is the top layer and the other layers sit under it.

  • An amber wash fills the land within 1 mile of a siren. A bright amber edge marks the mile. Land outside the edge stays dark, so the gaps show at once.
  • Quarter-mile rings are faint inside the mile and fade out soon after it.
  • Tornado paths are soft, translucent streaks under the coverage.
  • Place names sit in small clearings cut from the rings, with no boxes and no halos.
  • The lighter ground is the city that each percentage measures. A thin light line traces its limits.

Three ways to draw the city limits

In an early version, the lighter ground meant different things in the two maps. In Oklahoma City it was the city. In Tulsa it was ten towns. The Tulsa amber also included suburban sirens that the 81% does not count. I drew three versions with one rule for both maps.

One scale

Both maps use 33 pixels per mile in the 2,160-pixel poster. I checked this on the finished image, not only in the code. The 2-mile circle of an isolated siren measures 67 pixels across in each map, against 66 expected. The two downtowns are about 98 miles (157 km) apart, so a small map of Oklahoma shows where each map sits.

Limits

  • The Oklahoma City siren layer has no date. Sirens may have moved or been added since it was made.
  • The Tulsa file is from 2012.
  • The Oklahoma City map leaves out the suburban sirens.
  • The percentages are shares of land, not of people. Large parts of Oklahoma City are rural.
  • SPC paths are straight lines, and the recorded width applies along the whole path.
