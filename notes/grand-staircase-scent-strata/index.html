30 Day Map Challenge 2026 | Day 19: Smell

Scent Strata

Twelve plant communities of Utah's five national parks on one elevation scale, each with the smell that a published source gives for it. Above them is the Grand Staircase in 3D, colored by plant community.

ChallengeDay 19: Smell
SubjectArches, Canyonlands, Capitol Reef, Zion and Bryce Canyon, and the Grand Staircase from Kanab through Bryce Canyon
Made withBlender 5.2 (Cycles), Codex image generation (plant plates), Python (NumPy, pandas, Matplotlib)

The smell changes as you climb

The plants in Utah's national parks change with elevation. The smell of the air changes with them.

The poster puts 12 plant communities on one elevation scale. A plant community is a group of plants that grow together, such as pinyon and juniper woodland. Each bar covers the middle 80 percent of the community's land in the five parks. The dot on the bar is the mean elevation.

  • Tamarisk grows lowest, along the rivers, from 3,900 to 5,900 ft (1,189 to 1,798 m). Its flowers are fragrant.
  • Ponderosa pine grows from 5,800 to 8,300 ft (1,768 to 2,530 m). Its bark smells of vanilla.
  • Douglas-fir and white fir grow from 6,300 to 8,800 ft (1,920 to 2,682 m). Their needles smell of citrus when you break them.
  • Bristlecone and limber pine grow highest, from 7,900 to 8,900 ft (2,408 to 2,713 m).

The ranges overlap. Most communities grow across thousands of feet, so one elevation can have several smells.

Five more bars show where the land of each park is. Each bar covers the middle 80 percent of the park's area by elevation. Its colors show the community that covers the most land at each elevation:

  • Arches: 4,300 to 5,200 ft
  • Canyonlands: 4,200 to 5,900 ft
  • Capitol Reef: 5,200 to 7,000 ft
  • Zion: 4,500 to 7,200 ft
  • Bryce Canyon: 7,000 to 8,600 ft

Each smell comes from a source

I did not write the smell words. Each word comes from a published description of the plant. I searched each source page for the words before they went on the poster.

  • Tamarisk, fragrant flowers: NPS, Arches National Park.
  • Blackbrush, aromatic leaves: Virginia Tech Dendrology.
  • Shadscale, fishy: Virginia Tech Dendrology. The source says that crushed leaves may smell fishy.
  • Sand sage, pungent: Utah State University, Desert Plants of Utah.
  • Rabbitbrush, pineapple or rubber: USDA Forest Service, Plant of the Week. Some people smell pineapple, and others smell rubber.
  • Fremont cottonwood, fragrant buds: Utah State University Tree Browser.
  • Bare rock, rain-soaked dirt: NPS, monsoon season.
  • Pinyon, aromatic: Utah State University, range plants.
  • Big sagebrush, spicy and bitter: USDA Forest Service, Plant of the Week.
  • Ponderosa pine, vanilla bark: NPS, Bryce Canyon National Park.
  • Douglas-fir, citrus needles: NPS, Bryce Canyon National Park.
  • White fir, citrus needles: USDA NRCS plant guide.
  • Limber pine, turpentine: Flora of North America, volume 2. The source says that the fresh-cut wood has the odor of turpentine.

The bristlecone and limber pine community has the words "cut limber pine: turpentine". The source describes limber pine wood after a cut, not the living trees. I found no description of the smell of Great Basin bristlecone pine. The Flora of North America describes its resin, but not a smell.

For bigtooth maple and Gambel oak, the poster says "no published smell". I searched pages from the NPS, the USDA Forest Service and NRCS, university extension programs, Virginia Tech and the Flora of North America. None describes a smell for these plants.

The pinyon and juniper community has the words "aromatic pinyon". The source describes pinyon. I found no reliable description of the smell of Utah juniper.

The Grand Staircase

The Grand Staircase is a series of cliffs that step up from northern Arizona to the rim of Bryce Canyon.

The block runs 59 miles (96 km) north from northern Arizona, 7.5 miles (12 km) south of Kanab. It ends at the north end of Bryce Canyon National Park. It is 19 miles (30 km) deep. The terrain is USGS 3DEP elevation on a 40 m grid, with a vertical exaggeration of 4 times.

The front edge of the block is 2.7 miles (4.4 km) east of a straight line from Kanab to Rainbow Point. This puts all of Bryce Canyon National Park on the block. The white line is the park boundary. The block holds no part of Zion. The scent ladder uses all five parks.

The colors

Color shows one thing on the poster: the plant community. Each community has its own color, and the color comes from the plant. Rabbitbrush flowers are gold, maple leaves are red, and the firs are blue.

I tested the colors with simulations of the three common types of color blindness. Some pairs of colors are close. So each bar also has the name of its community or park, and each plate has its name.

On the block, each 40 m cell shows the most common community in the 600 m (about 2,000 ft) square around it. This removes small patches that would look like noise at this scale.

What Codex did

Codex image generation, a tool from OpenAI, drew the 12 plant plates. No image from Codex has text in it. Python sets every word, number and the elevation scale. Blender rendered the block directly from the data, with no change by Codex.

Inside the scripts

Python scripts prepare the data and make the poster.

  • day19_utah5_elev.py pairs land cover and elevation for each park.
  • day19_block_fetch.py gets the terrain and land cover for the block.
  • day19_scent_drape.py colors the top of the block by community.
  • day19_blender_block.py renders the block.
  • day19_strata_v10.py makes the poster.

Method

  • Land cover is the LANDFIRE 2025 Existing Vegetation Type. Elevation is USGS 3DEP. Both are on one 30 m grid for each park, in an equal-area projection (EPSG:5070).
  • The vegetation types go into 12 communities. A community name such as "shadscale and sand sage" gives the main plants of the group.
  • The range and the mean elevation of a community use all of its land in the five parks.
  • The block uses 3DEP terrain and LANDFIRE 2025 on a 40 m grid. Blender's Cycles renderer makes the image.
  • The poster is 2160 by 2700 pixels. The page shows the same image.

Limits

  • A smell word describes the main plant of a community. Other plants grow there too. Rabbitbrush stands for a shrub and grass community where rabbitbrush is common. The sagebrush source describes big sagebrush, but low sagebrush also grows in that community.
  • The smell of pinyon and juniper woodland comes from the pinyon source only. The turpentine smell is for cut limber pine wood only.
  • LANDFIRE maps vegetation from satellite images and models. Some small areas have the wrong type.
  • A bar shows the middle 80 percent of a community's land. Some of each community grows above or below its bar.
  • The colors on the block show the most common community in each 600 m square. Small stands of other communities do not show.

Data and credit

The U.S. Geological Survey 3D Elevation Program provides the terrain. LANDFIRE, a program of the USDA Forest Service and the U.S. Department of the Interior, provides the vegetation types. The park boundaries come from the National Park Service Land Resources Division. The smell sources are in the list above.
