Scent Strata

Twelve plant communities of Utah's five national parks, each at its mean elevation, with the smell that a published source gives for it. Above them is the Grand Staircase in 3D, colored by the age of its rock.

The smell changes as you climb

The plants in Utah's national parks change with elevation. The smell of the air changes with them.

The poster puts 12 plant communities on one elevation scale. A plant community is a group of plants that grow together, such as pinyon and juniper woodland. Each community sits at its mean elevation across the five parks.

  • Tamarisk grows lowest, along the rivers, at a mean of 4,640 ft (1,414 m). Its flowers are fragrant.
  • Ponderosa pine is at 7,090 ft (2,161 m). Its bark smells of vanilla.
  • Douglas-fir and white fir are at 7,680 ft (2,341 m). Their needles smell of citrus when you break them.
  • Bristlecone and limber pine are highest, at 8,480 ft (2,585 m).

Five white bars show where the land of each park is. Each bar covers the middle 80 percent of the park's area by elevation:

  • Arches: 4,300 to 5,200 ft
  • Canyonlands: 4,200 to 5,900 ft
  • Capitol Reef: 5,200 to 7,000 ft
  • Zion: 4,500 to 7,200 ft
  • Bryce Canyon: 7,000 to 8,600 ft

Each smell comes from a source

I did not write the smell words. Each word comes from a published description of the plant. I searched each source page for the words before they went on the poster.

  • Tamarisk, fragrant flowers: NPS, Arches National Park.
  • Blackbrush, aromatic leaves: Virginia Tech Dendrology.
  • Shadscale, fishy: Virginia Tech Dendrology. The source says that crushed leaves may smell fishy.
  • Sand sage, pungent: Utah State University, Desert Plants of Utah.
  • Rabbitbrush, pineapple or rubber: USDA Forest Service, Plant of the Week. Some people smell pineapple, and others smell rubber.
  • Fremont cottonwood, fragrant buds: Utah State University Tree Browser.
  • Bare rock, rain-soaked dirt: NPS, monsoon season.
  • Pinyon, aromatic: Utah State University, range plants.
  • Big sagebrush, spicy and bitter: USDA Forest Service, Plant of the Week.
  • Ponderosa pine, vanilla bark: NPS, Bryce Canyon National Park.
  • Douglas-fir, citrus needles: NPS, Bryce Canyon National Park.
  • White fir, citrus needles: USDA NRCS plant guide.

Two communities have no smell word. For bristlecone and limber pine, the only source that I found was a chemistry study of the gases that the trees release. It does not describe a smell that a person notices. For bigtooth maple and Gambel oak, I found no source.

The pinyon and juniper community has the words "aromatic pinyon". The source describes pinyon. I found no reliable description of the smell of Utah juniper.

The Grand Staircase

The Grand Staircase is a series of cliffs that step up from northern Arizona to the rim of Bryce Canyon. Each step is a younger layer of rock.

The block runs about 41 miles (66.4 km) from south of Kanab to Rainbow Point in Bryce Canyon. It is 12 miles (20 km) deep. The terrain is USGS 3DEP elevation on a 40 m grid, with a vertical exaggeration of 4 times.

The block shows the land between the parks, not the parks themselves. It holds 5 percent of Bryce Canyon National Park and no part of Zion. The scent ladder uses all five parks.

The layers of rock

The colors of the block show the age of the rock. A geologic map shows the rock at the surface only. The colors inside the block come from a simple model.

  1. A script samples the mapped rock every 240 m (790 ft) along the front edge of the block. There are 287 samples. The map units come from Macrostrat. Most come from the Utah Geological Survey map of the Kanab 30' x 60' quadrangle.
  2. Each map unit goes into one of seven groups by its formation name. The groups are Kaibab, the Chocolate, Vermilion and White Cliffs, the Carmel slopes, and the Gray and Pink Cliffs.
  3. Each contact between two groups is a flat surface. It goes through the mapped change along the front edge and dips north at 72 ft per kilometer.
  4. The top of the Kaibab is not on the map along this line. The model puts it 1,700 ft below the top of the Chocolate Cliffs group.

This "layer cake" puts 168 of 202 mapped samples on the front edge in the correct group. That is 83 percent. On the top of the block, 50 of 61 mapped samples agree, or 82 percent. Most errors are where layers interfinger, such as the thin tongues of Navajo Sandstone.

What Codex did

Blender rendered the block with the model colors. Then Codex image generation, a tool from OpenAI, redrew the render in a screen-print style. It added hatching, stipple, lines of bedding and tree marks. Codex reported that it changed some terrain details and colors a little. So the block on the poster is an illustration of the data, not an exact map.

Codex also drew the 12 plant plates. No image from Codex has text in it. Python sets every word, number and the elevation scale.

Inside the scripts

Python scripts prepare the data and make the poster. day19_utah5_elev.py pairs land cover and elevation for each park. day19_geo_drape.py builds the layer cake. day19_blender_block.py renders the block. day19_strata_final.py makes the poster.

Method

  • Land cover is the LANDFIRE 2025 Existing Vegetation Type. Elevation is USGS 3DEP. Both are on one 30 m grid for each park, in an equal-area projection (EPSG:5070).
  • The vegetation types go into 12 communities. A community name such as "shadscale and sand sage" gives the main plants of the group.
  • The mean elevation of a community uses all of its land in the five parks.
  • The block uses 3DEP terrain and LANDFIRE 2025 on a 40 m grid along a line from Kanab to Rainbow Point. Blender's Cycles renderer makes the image.
  • The poster is 2160 by 2700 pixels. The page shows the same image.

Limits

  • A smell word describes the main plant of a community. Other plants grow there too. Rabbitbrush stands for a shrub and grass community where rabbitbrush is common. The sagebrush source describes big sagebrush, but low sagebrush also grows in that community.
  • The smell of pinyon and juniper woodland comes from the pinyon source only.
  • LANDFIRE maps vegetation from satellite images and models. Some small areas have the wrong type.
  • The layer cake is a simple model. Real contacts bend, and some layers thin out.
  • Codex redrew the block. The colors and forest marks follow the data, but small terrain details can differ.
  • A mean elevation is not a range. Most communities grow across thousands of feet.

Data and credit

The U.S. Geological Survey 3D Elevation Program provides the terrain. LANDFIRE, a program of the USDA Forest Service and the U.S. Department of the Interior, provides the vegetation types. Macrostrat serves the geologic map units. They come from the Utah Geological Survey map of the Kanab 30' x 60' quadrangle (Doelling, 2008) and the NPS Geologic Resources Inventory map of Bryce Canyon National Park (2010). The smell sources are in the list above.
