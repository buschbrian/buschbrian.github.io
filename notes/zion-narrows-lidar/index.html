30 Day Map Challenge 2026 | Day 14: Borgesian Map

The Zion Narrows in Lidar

A view down into the Narrows of Zion National Park, drawn from U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) lidar points. The slot canyon is a maze that you walk in the river.

ChallengeDay 14: Borgesian Map
SubjectThe Narrows, from the Temple of Sinawava to Orderville Canyon
Made withBlender 5.2 (Cycles), Python (laspy, NumPy, Matplotlib, GeoPandas)
Challenge dateNovember 14, 2026

A maze that you walk in the river

Jorge Luis Borges wrote many stories about labyrinths. In one very short piece, he also wrote about a map as large as the empire that it shows.

The Narrows connects to both ideas. It is the slot canyon of the North Fork Virgin River. The walls close in on both sides, and the trail is the river itself.

Lidar is a laser survey from an aircraft. It records the ground as millions of points. This survey has about 2 points for each square foot. That is not a map at the scale of the empire, but it is closer than most maps.

The walk

The map follows about 2.5 miles of trail, as OpenStreetMap maps it. The walk starts at the Temple of Sinawava. The paved Riverside Walk ends after 0.9 miles. From there, the route is in the river. Orderville Canyon joins at 2.5 miles.

In the narrowest part below Orderville, the walls of the slot rise about 650 to 700 feet above the river. The rims are 1,000 to 1,200 feet above the river.

From points to a picture

Four USGS lidar tiles cover the canyon. They hold 209 million points. The block on the map holds 108.8 million of them, after noise is removed. The block is about 1 mile by 1.9 miles.

Blender draws each point as a small sphere. The color shows the height above the river:

  • Blue-gray is at the water.
  • Warm red is low on the walls.
  • A pale color is near the rims.
  • Green marks trees and shrubs.

The laser looks down, so steep walls get fewer points than flat ground. At first, the dark background showed through the gaps. An elevation model is a grid of ground heights. A 1 m elevation model now sits 2.5 m inside the rock, behind the points. The gaps show shadowed rock.

Two straight lines

The first full render showed two straight lines that ran up the frame. They were not in the canyon.

The aircraft flew the survey in overlapping strips. Where two strips overlap, the points differ a little in brightness and height. Each strip edge became a line in the render.

The lidar files mark these overlap points. In the block, 67% of the points have the mark. When I removed all of them, the lines went away. But the steep walls lost their detail, because the second strip carries much of it.

So the final map keeps a share of the overlap points that depends on the slope. Below 45 degrees, it keeps none. Above 70 degrees, it keeps all of them. Between the two, the share rises smoothly. The map draws 51.5 million points.

Inside the scripts

Three Python scripts build the map. day14_points_prep.py reads the lidar tiles with laspy. day14_points_view.py builds the scene in Blender and renders it. day14_still.py adds the labels, the panels and the insets.

The scripts pass the default checks of the ruff linter and use its code format. The excerpts below are trimmed. A # ... line marks a cut.

How to read the map

  • The white dotted line is the hike, as OpenStreetMap maps it.
  • Each label gives the distance along the trail from the Temple of Sinawava.
  • The river is dark because water returns almost no lidar.
  • A point is green only when its laser pulse had more than one return. Part of the pulse hit leaves, and part went through.
  • The orange line A–B marks the cross-section. The cross-section shows a 66 ft slice of points across the slot, with no vertical exaggeration.
  • The small map at the lower right shows where the view is in Zion Canyon.

Method

  • The lidar is the USGS 3D Elevation Program project UT_ZionNP_QL2_2016, in four tiles. Points in the noise classes are removed.
  • The camera is 2,700 m above the river, south of the block. It looks north and down into the slot.
  • Blender's Cycles renderer makes the image at 2160 by 2700 pixels. A light haze darkens the far ground a little.
  • The labels use the camera of the render. A line-of-sight test on the 1 m elevation model finds the places that the walls hide.
  • Trail distances come from the OpenStreetMap trail lines. The full route is 4,031 m, or 2.5 miles.
  • Wall heights come from the 1 m elevation model at the cross-section. The slot walls are the highest ground within 150 m of the river. The rims are the highest ground within 300 m.

Data notes

  • The real walk is longer than the mapped line, because the route crosses the river many times.
  • The National Park Service gives rounded distances. It describes the Riverside Walk as about 1 mile.
  • Airborne lidar looks down. It records fewer points on vertical walls, and it cannot see under overhangs.
  • The cross-section shows one slice. Other parts of the canyon are narrower or wider.

Data and credit
