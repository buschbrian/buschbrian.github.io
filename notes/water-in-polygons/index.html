Water in Polygons

The same western landscape, read through snow, precipitation, soil moisture and drought.

Polygons are the units of comparison

A drainage area is land that drains toward a common outlet. These maps use six-digit hydrologic units, called HUC-6 basins. Their boundaries come from the U.S. Geological Survey Watershed Boundary Dataset.

The colors answer a different question in each scene. Snow compares mountain sites with their normal snow water. Precipitation shows recent gauge totals. Soil moisture shows a weekly change at sensors. Drought shows the U.S. Drought Monitor’s assessment of land conditions.

The observations cover different dates and station sets. They do not form a water budget or trace where one storm’s water went.

Explore the Western Water Dashboard.

A snow season in nine observations

The first scene steps from January 1 to June 17, 2026. Every map shows an observed day from the completed 2025–26 season. The color compares summed snow water at reporting sites with summed normal snow water at those sites.

Normal is the median for the same date in 1991–2020. Each basin needs enough reporting sites for a fair value. A hatched basin has too little normal snow to compare. Gray means no value.

Precipitation adds up over days

The second scene changes the window from one day to three days to seven days. All three windows end on November 1, 2026. The gauges measure rain and the water equivalent of caught snow.

A basin takes its color from the mean at stations with valid readings at both ends. A falling gauge total is excluded. These mountain stations do not measure every part of the land.

Soil moisture changes over a week

The third scene shows seven-day change in soil water, ending November 1, 2026. Thirty-four of 49 basins have a usable change. Three hundred stations contribute.

A station needs sensors at three depths: 2 inches or 4 inches, 8 inches, and 20 inches. The dashboard averages the three depths. Frozen readings are excluded. Basin fills show the mean change at reporting stations.

Drought has a shape

The fourth scene shows the U.S. Drought Monitor map for October 27, 2026. Twenty-one of 75 basins contain extreme or exceptional drought. Ten basins have partial U.S. coverage.

The drought polygons cross basin boundaries. They describe an expert assessment that combines several kinds of evidence. The basin outlines identify the units used for the dashboard’s figures.

What changed in one week

The last map compares October 20 with October 27. Nineteen basins have less measured land at severe drought or worse, called D2 or worse. None have more. The other 56 did not change.

Symbol guide

  • Polygon outlines mark HUC-6 drainage basins.
  • Snow and precipitation use the dashboard’s brown-to-blue classes. Their units and thresholds differ.
  • Soil moisture uses brown for drier, a pale neutral for steady, and blue for wetter.
  • Drought classes use the monitor’s yellow-to-maroon scale. D0 means abnormally dry; D1 through D4 are increasing drought severity.
  • Weekly drought change uses green for less land at D2 or worse and pink for more.
  • Gray and hatching retain the missing-value rules stated in each key.

How the video is made

Chrome DevTools MCP controls the published dashboard. A temporary poster layout wraps its ArcGIS maps during capture. The script moves the snow date slider and the existing precipitation and drought controls.

Python and the shared motion kit assemble the captures. FFmpeg encodes a square video at 60 frames per second and a vertical Reel at 30. This page uses a square 30-frame-per-second cut without the end card.

The snow scene shows nine selected observations. Values switch with the observed map. Only the progress indicator and transitions ease. No numeric value is interpolated, and missing observations remain missing.

The video starts when the reader selects Play. The video has no audio. The tables, stills and captions describe its observations.
