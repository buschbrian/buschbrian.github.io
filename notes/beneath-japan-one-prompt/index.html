30 Day Map Challenge 2026 | Day 10: Prompting

One Prompt, Six Maps of Japan

A shared brief gave six models 30 minutes each to make a complete map. All six finished within the limit. One review of all six found one factual error, and text too small for a phone on every poster.

From scripts to the whole map

Claude and Codex had helped with scripts, Python, and downloads on earlier challenge days. Those prompts usually took several attempts.

For Day 10, I asked GPT-6 Astra, on Extra High, to write one prompt. Each model had to make a professional map within 30 minutes. On November 5 the brief went to Claude Opus 5.5 on High and GPT-6.1 Sol on Extra High. On November 6 the same text went, unchanged, to GPT-6.1 Terra, Claude Sonnet 5.5, Claude Haiku 5.5, and GPT-6 Luna.

I gave no follow-up instructions during any run. Each session had one human message: the pasted brief. The models could inspect and revise their own work. “One shot” describes the human input, not a ban on internal revisions.

The brief: Beneath Japan

The question was specific: where did earthquakes occur around Japan in 2025, and what does their depth reveal?

  • Use USGS earthquakes of magnitude 4.5 or greater, within 128–147°E and 29–46°N.
  • Use the 2025 calendar year in UTC. Exclude January 1, 2026.
  • Pair a map with a longitude-versus-depth plot for the 36–40°N band. This shows where earthquakes lie below the surface.
  • Include a title, legends, scale, orientation, projection, sources, and a clear explanation.
  • Deliver a 2160 × 2700 poster, a 540 × 675 preview, an editable vector export, code, data, and documentation.
  • Count planning, downloads, rendering, checks, and delivery within the 30-minute limit.

The brief fixed the question and evidence. Each model chose its tools, visual hierarchy, colors, and supporting context.

What the checks supported

Claude Fable 5.1 reviewed all six posters on one rubric. It made none of the maps. It recomputed every number from each run’s saved data, re-ran the four November 6 scripts, and measured text heights in the previews. It had read every run’s files before it scored the posters, so the scoring was informed rather than blind.

For Opus and Sol, two earlier reviews also apply. Astra, which wrote the brief, reviewed both on November 5. Fable then reviewed them without relying on that audit, and a Codex follow-up checked Fable’s findings against the files.

  • All six runs used the same 515 earthquake records. Every number printed on every poster recomputes from the saved data.
  • 449 earthquakes, about 87%, were shallower than 70 km. Fourteen were at least 300 km deep. The deepest was 561 km below the surface. The largest was magnitude 7.6, on December 8, 2025.
  • Every poster has the required elements. Two are partial: Luna has no annotation at a pattern, and the band shading named in Terra’s legend does not appear.
  • The four November 6 runs render their posters again pixel for pixel from their own folders. Sol’s folder does the same. The Opus run needed files outside its folder.
  • No factual error appears on the four November 6 posters. The one error class from November 5, a misattributed citation, did not recur.

Matching a saved image proves repeatability in that environment. It does not prove the science, design, or delivery package is complete.

The issues that remained

A factual credit error. Opus named “Tarr et al.” for a USGS report whose first author is Susan Rhea. The correct citation is Rhea et al. (2010). The mistake appears in the poster and its documentation. The other five posters cite the subduction explanation by URL or DOI, by an exact author list, or by agency name alone.

Text that needed more room, on every poster. Each poster has body text under 6 px at the required preview size. Haiku’s subtitle and Terra’s “449 of 515” are the only observations that read at phone size among the four later runs. A full-resolution export cannot substitute for a phone-size check.

A rendering defect. Luna’s projected clip polygon does not fill its frame. A slanted wedge of bare paper shows at the top left and a triangle at the top right.

A legend that does not match its symbols. Luna’s depth legend turns fully yellow at about 300 km and stays yellow to 600 km. Its map symbols use the full 0 to 600 km range, so only the deepest event, at 561 km, is yellow. Astra found this in its blind review. Fable did not.

Missing annotations. The brief asked for two annotations at specific patterns in the data. Luna put three statistics in its legend column and none on the map or the depth section.

A source not named on the poster. Haiku credits its subduction interpretation as “USGS; see README”. Its README cites a USGS event page and a USGS poster, both real, read only through search excerpts.

A portability gap. Opus’s script used basemaps, plate boundaries, and fonts outside its delivered folder. It ran on the original machine, but the folder alone was insufficient for a clean-machine rebuild. The other five folders are self-contained.

A narrow geographic description. Opus described deep events as south of Honshu and near Vladivostok. Some also lay beneath Honshu and west of the Noto Peninsula. The explanation needed broader wording.

Design tradeoffs. Sol and Luna used a simpler coastline that omits small islands near the Tokara cluster. Sol, Haiku, and Luna used continuous depth scales that group most shallow earthquakes into similar colors. Terra hedged its interpretation to “one explanation”. Sonnet used a typeface that renders differently outside macOS. Each scale bar is true at a stated latitude and within 2.3% across Japan. These are improvements to consider, rather than failed requirements.

My scores also weigh the order of the depth colors. The usual order runs light at the surface to dark at depth. Opus, Sonnet, and Terra follow it. Sol, Haiku, and Luna reverse it, so their deepest earthquakes are the lightest marks.

The reviews needed checking too

Fable questioned Sol’s 500 km scale bar after measuring about 498.1 km in projected coordinates. A projection converts the curved Earth to a flat map. Its coordinate distances can differ from ground distances.

The follow-up measured the bar on Earth: its two segments totalled 500.0003 km. The local ground scale was correct. Opus’s 400 km bar measured about 399.6 km, a negligible difference for this poster.

Other criticisms were preferences rather than broken requirements. The brief did not mandate a particular Python package manager. A valid depth scale did not have to make deeper events darker. Neither issue warranted a failure.

That distinction matters. A confident review is another claim to check, especially when it alleges a technical error.

What AI can check, and what people still decide

AI helped compare records, recompute statistics, inspect coordinate transforms, measure scale, and rerun the code. These tasks had saved inputs and observable results.

Human review still needs to ask whether the map tells a supported story. The reader must be able to find the main point, read the symbols, and understand the limits. Credits and packaging also need verification.

A second AI review can help find omissions. It does not replace the mapmaker’s decision to publish. In this experiment, the reviewers performed the technical audits; I set the task and asked for the follow-up checks.

The review in 70 seconds

I expected more problems from a single prompt. All six maps were coherent, their numbers held up, and all six finished within the limit. The problems that remained were about reading, not arithmetic: text too small for a phone on every poster, one wrong author list, one bare corner. A strong brief reduced the work needed after delivery. It did not remove the need for review.

Sources and scope

This is a selected earthquake catalog, not a hazard forecast or a map of every earthquake. The magnitude threshold, geographic bounds, year, and profile band limit the story.

  • USGS earthquake catalog and query service. All six runs saved the source records used in their maps.
  • Rhea et al. (2010), Seismicity of the Earth 1900–2007, Japan and vicinity. USGS Open-File Report 2010–1083–D, DOI 10.3133/ofr20101083D. Cited by Opus, Sol, Terra, and Luna.
  • Hayes et al. (2018), Slab2, a comprehensive subduction zone geometry model. Science 362(6410):58–61. Cited by Sonnet, with the USGS Slab2 data release.
  • USGS explanation of earthquake depth.
  • Natural Earth provides basemap data. Sol and Luna used the simpler 1:50m coastline, which drops small islands.
  • PB2002 plate boundaries, used by Opus for tectonic context.

Why the ocean is cream

The Grok script meant to draw navy water and cream land. The small locator map shows those colors. On the main map, the water is cream.

The script keeps each Natural Earth land record that touches the map area. One record also holds Antarctica. The poster uses a Lambert conic projection, which flattens the Earth onto a cone. That projection spreads the Antarctica shape across the whole frame, and the land color covers the sea.

Grok inspected its export and did not report the problem. Its documentation does not mention it.
