30 Day Map Challenge 2026 | Day 10: Prompting

One Prompt, Two Maps of Japan

A shared brief gave two models 30 minutes to make a complete map. Both finished early. The review found fewer problems than I expected.

ChallengeDay 10: Prompting
Subject2025 earthquakes around Japan
ModelsClaude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6.1 Sol
Challenge dateNovember 10, 2026

From scripts to the whole map

Claude and Codex had helped with scripts, Python, and downloads on earlier challenge days. Those prompts usually took several attempts.

For Day 10, I asked GPT-6 Astra, on Extra High, to write one prompt for both models. Each model had to make a professional map within 30 minutes. The same brief went to Claude Opus 5.5 on High and GPT-6.1 Sol on Extra High.

I gave no follow-up instructions during either run. The models could inspect and revise their own work. “One shot” describes the human input, not a ban on internal revisions.

The brief: Beneath Japan

The question was specific: where did earthquakes occur around Japan in 2025, and what does their depth reveal?

  • Use USGS earthquakes of magnitude 4.5 or greater, within 128–147°E and 29–46°N.
  • Use the 2025 calendar year in UTC. Exclude January 1, 2026.
  • Pair a map with a longitude-versus-depth plot for the 36–40°N band. This shows where earthquakes lie below the surface.
  • Include a title, legends, scale, orientation, projection, sources, and a clear explanation.
  • Deliver a 2160 × 2700 poster, a 540 × 675 preview, an editable vector export, code, data, and documentation.
  • Count planning, downloads, rendering, checks, and delivery within the 30-minute limit.

The brief fixed the question and evidence. Each model chose its tools, visual hierarchy, colors, and supporting context.

Two different posters

Opus included more geological context. Named trenches and depth classes helped explain the earthquake pattern. Sol gave more space to the map and its main observations. Its reading order worked better for a social post.

What the checks supported

I asked Astra to review both outputs, then asked for an independent Fable 5.1 review. A follow-up checked Fable’s findings against the files and calculations.

Astra had also written the prompt. Its first review was therefore not independent of the brief’s design. Fable provided another perspective, and caught a citation error that the first review missed.

  • Both maps used the same 515 unique earthquake records. The shared depth-profile band contained 165 events.
  • 449 earthquakes, about 87%, were shallower than 70 km. Fourteen were at least 300 km deep.
  • The deepest event was 561.251 km below the surface. The largest was magnitude 7.6, on December 8, 2025.
  • The plotted coordinates, depth values, legend calculations, and checked numerical claims matched the saved data.
  • Both render scripts reproduced their original PNGs pixel for pixel in the available environment.

The checks found no consequential numerical or coordinate errors in either map. Matching a saved image proves repeatability in that environment. It does not prove the science, design, or delivery package is complete.

The issues that remained

A factual credit error. Opus named “Tarr et al.” for a USGS report whose first author is Susan Rhea. The correct citation is Rhea et al. (2010). The mistake appears in the poster and its documentation.

A portability gap. Opus’s script used basemaps, plate boundaries, and fonts outside its delivered folder. It ran on the original machine, but the folder alone was insufficient for a clean-machine rebuild. Sol included its geographic inputs.

Text that needed more room. Much of Opus’s sidebar and profile explanation became tiny at the required preview size. Both posters had small legends or credits. A full-resolution export cannot substitute for a phone-size check.

A narrow geographic description. Opus described deep events as south of Honshu and near Vladivostok. Some also lay beneath Honshu and west of the Noto Peninsula. The explanation needed broader wording.

Design tradeoffs in Sol’s map. Its simpler coastline omitted small islands near the Tokara cluster. Its continuous 0–600 km depth scale grouped most shallow earthquakes into similar pinks. Trench lines could have strengthened the geological explanation. These are improvements to consider, rather than failed requirements.

The reviews needed checking too

Fable questioned Sol’s 500 km scale bar after measuring about 498.1 km in projected coordinates. A projection converts the curved Earth to a flat map. Its coordinate distances can differ from ground distances.

The follow-up measured the bar on Earth: its two segments totalled 500.0003 km. The local ground scale was correct. Opus’s 400 km bar measured about 399.6 km, a negligible difference for this poster.

Other criticisms were preferences rather than broken requirements. The brief did not mandate a particular Python package manager. A valid depth scale did not have to make deeper events darker. Neither issue warranted a failure.

That distinction matters. A confident review is another claim to check, especially when it alleges a technical error.

What AI can check, and what people still decide

AI helped compare records, recompute statistics, inspect coordinate transforms, measure scale, and rerun the code. These tasks had saved inputs and observable results.

Human review still needs to ask whether the map tells a supported story. The reader must be able to find the main point, read the symbols, and understand the limits. Credits and packaging also need verification.

A second AI review can help find omissions. It does not replace the mapmaker’s decision to publish. In this experiment, the reviewers performed the technical audits; I set the task and asked for the follow-up checks.

The process in 98 seconds

I expected more problems from a single prompt. Both maps were coherent, their core numbers held up, and both finished early. The remaining issues still mattered. A strong brief reduced the work needed after delivery; it did not remove the need for review.

Sources and scope

This is a selected earthquake catalog, not a hazard forecast or a map of every earthquake. The magnitude threshold, geographic bounds, year, and profile band limit the story.

  • USGS earthquake catalog and query service. Both runs saved the source records used in their maps.
  • Rhea et al. (2010), Seismicity of the Earth 1900–2007, Japan and vicinity. USGS Open-File Report 2010–1083–D, DOI 10.3133/ofr20101083D.
  • USGS explanation of earthquake depth.
  • Natural Earth provides basemap data. Opus used finer coastlines than Sol.
  • PB2002 plate boundaries, used by Opus for tectonic context.
