30 Day Map Challenge 2026 | Day 6: Vintage

Holmes's 1882 Zion Panorama, Redrawn

W. H. Holmes’s panorama of Zion was published in 1882. I estimated a likely viewpoint from his skyline, then redrew the view from elevation data in his style.

ChallengeDay 6: Vintage
SubjectZion from the ridge south of the Virgin River, after W. H. Holmes
Made withPython (NumPy, SciPy, Matplotlib, contourpy), Blender
Challenge dateNovember 6, 2026

The plate

In 1882, Clarence Dutton published a monograph on the geology of the Grand Cañon District. Its atlas holds panoramas by the artist W. H. Holmes. One plate, "The Temples and Towers of the Virgen," looks north across the Virgin River to the West Temple, in what is now Zion National Park.

Against today's elevation data, the plate holds up well. The ridgelines align closely. The fit suggests Holmes drew the West Temple about a quarter taller than its modeled height.

Then and now

The board sets the plate above a render from the estimated viewpoint. The render is bare earth: it shows the ground with no trees or buildings. The model does not resolve fine details such as junipers and individual rock blocks.

Finding the viewpoint

Python scripts do the work, with NumPy for the math and Matplotlib for the drawing. day06_station_search.py finds the viewpoint. day06_hatch_proof.py holds the camera, the ray march and the pen lines. day06_panorama.py adds the tint, the river and the labels. The scripts pass the default checks of the ruff linter and use its code format. The excerpts below are trimmed. A # ... line marks a cut.

Dutton's text puts the view at "the crest of the isthmus," past Smithsonian Butte. That gives a ridge, not a point. The plate's own skyline gives the rest.

  • A short script traced the skyline in a 1,600 px copy of the plate, one point every 4 px.
  • A digital elevation model (DEM) is a grid of ground heights. The search used the USGS 3DEP 10 m DEM.
  • From about 1,000 candidate points around the butte, the search computed the skyline from the DEM.
  • For each candidate, a least-squares fit matched the DEM skyline to the plate's skyline.
  • The best fits lie on the ridge crest, 3 km southeast of the summit. The fit misses by 8 px root mean square on the plate copy, about half a degree.

Nearby points fit almost as well, so the estimated viewpoint is uncertain by a few hundred meters.

The fit searches two numbers and solves for two more. The outer loops try each horizontal scale and center bearing. For each pair, a weighted linear regression gives the vertical scale and the horizon row directly. The ratio of the two scales is the height stretch. The search rejects any stretch outside 0.8 to 3.

The fit also estimates Holmes's height proportions. His skyline as a whole fits at 0.96 times the modeled height. The West Temple fits at 1.25 to 1.3 times its modeled height. The drawing uses 1.25 and says so.

Drawing in his hatching

The drawing uses a level camera with a cylindrical projection. Each screen column is one bearing. Each row is one angle above or below the horizon. The camera stands 1.7 m above the ground at the viewpoint and spans 104 degrees, as the plate does.

The script marches out along each column, from near to far. It keeps the highest screen point so far, a "floating horizon." Ground below that line is hidden. Screen rows count down from the top, so the highest point is the smallest row number. One cumulative minimum gives the horizon for a whole block of columns at once.

The first proofs stood on the summit of Smithsonian Butte, which was a guess. They put diagonal hatching on every slope, with no bedding lines, and the foreground broke into patches.

Holmes drew cliffs as horizontal bedding lines with short vertical strokes stacked between them. The script follows that structure:

  • DEM contours become the bedding lines, on faces of 30 degrees and steeper.
  • Short strokes hang from each contour toward the next, on faces of 25 degrees and steeper. They sit closer together in shade.
  • Mesa tops and flats stay mostly blank, with a few short dashes.
  • A flat tint marks shaded faces, like the plate's second tint. Its color comes from the scan.
  • Inside 1.2 km, the march and the lines use 1 m lidar. Lidar is a laser survey flown from an aircraft.

The lines must stay about 8 px apart on screen at every distance. A contour interval that works at 1 km piles up into a dark mass at 30 km. So the interval doubles with distance, from 5 m to 320 m.

The river comes from the 10 m DEM too. A priority flood from the edge of the DEM routes water through pits and flats in one pass. From the crest, the main stem of the Virgin River stays hidden in its inner canyon. About 5 km of the North Fork shows, 9 to 17 km out, on the floor of Zion Canyon.

A second check in Blender

I built the same view in Blender with the Cycles renderer. Its panoramic camera matches the script's camera, and the meshes have the curve of the earth built in.

The two methods find the skyline in different ways: Blender by ray tracing, the script by its column march. They agree within 0.17 px in 95% of columns. The worst column differs by about half a pixel.

Blender's Freestyle line tool does not work with panoramic cameras, so the pen lines stay in Python. The Blender render at true heights is the lower panel of the then-and-now board.

Symbol guide

  • Horizontal lines are contours on steep faces, drawn as bedding lines.
  • Short vertical strokes hang between them. They sit closer together in shade.
  • A pale olive tint marks shaded faces.
  • Short dashes mark visible flats.
  • Blue marks the stretch of the North Fork of the Virgin River that is in view.
  • Labels name Smithsonian Butte, West Temple, Zion Canyon, East Temple and Eagle Crags at their GNIS bearings.
  • The drawing stretches heights by a quarter. The render on the board uses true heights.
Watch the redraw (30 seconds)

The plate fades while lines draw the view from the skyline forward. A wipe compares the two views. The drawing then stretches heights by a quarter. A pale tint marks shaded faces, and place names appear. The ending shows Holmes’s plate above the finished redraw.

Narration transcript

I redrew Holmes’s eighteen eighty-two view of Zion using modern elevation data.

It starts with the skyline, then works forward, line by line.

Look at how closely the ridgelines match. He drew the temples about a quarter taller.

I kept that stretch and added a pale tint to bring out the shaded faces.

Data notes

  • The elevation data is bare earth. It does not resolve the plate's junipers or individual rock blocks.
  • The estimated viewpoint is uncertain by a few hundred meters.
  • The river line is traced from the 10 m DEM. It does not come from a mapped stream layer.
  • In 1.8% of columns, the skyline ends at the edge of the 45 km elevation grid, 40 km or more out. No break shows there.
  • Holmes's caption names "the opening of the Parunuweap" at the far right. No GNIS point fits that bearing, so the drawing has no label there.

Data and credit

The U.S. Geological Survey provides the 3DEP elevation data: the 10 m DEM and 1 m lidar flown from 2017 to 2020. Peak names come from the USGS Geographic Names Information System (GNIS).

The plate is W. H. Holmes, "The Temples and Towers of the Virgen," Atlas Sheet IV, in C. E.
