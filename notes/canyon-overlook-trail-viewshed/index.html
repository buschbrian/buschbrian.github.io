What the Canyon Overlook Trail Sees

A viewshed every 5 m along a short trail in Zion National Park. The trail keeps most of its view until the last few steps.

ChallengeDay 5: Sight
SubjectCanyon Overlook Trail, Zion National Park
Made withPython (NumPy, SciPy, GeoPandas, Matplotlib), GDAL
Challenge dateNovember 5, 2026

A late view

The Canyon Overlook Trail is a walk of about 770 m to a viewpoint above Pine Creek. This map models what a hiker can see from each point on the way.

On bare earth, the first 500 m see less than a tenth of the ground that is in view at the rim. About two-thirds of the way along, a gap opens a window west to the West Temple and the Sundial. Then the rock closes in again.

Nearly half of all the ground the walk shows comes into view only in the last 15 m. That ground includes the canyon right below the viewpoint: Pine Creek and the highway switchbacks.

Watch the walk

How the model works

A viewshed is the area that is visible from one point. The model runs a viewshed every 5 m along the trail, 155 positions in all.

  • The terrain is a digital elevation model (DEM), a grid of ground heights. The base is the USGS 3DEP 10 m DEM, resampled to 2 m cells.
  • Near the viewpoint, the model uses 1 m lidar from a 2016 survey of Zion. Lidar is a laser survey flown from an aircraft.
  • The 10 m cells smear the cliff below the viewpoint into a slope. A slope is visible where a cliff is not, so the lidar matters here.
  • The elevation data is bare earth. It shows the ground with no trees or buildings.
  • The trail line comes from the National Park Service's archived 2014 trail dataset. The eye sits 1.7 m above the lidar ground at each position.
  • Each viewshed reaches 8 km. From the viewpoint, no ground beyond 8 km is in view.
  • Each 2 m cell keeps the first distance along the walk at which it comes into view. Ground first in view in the last 15 m is "only at the rim."

From one viewpoint to the whole walk

The first version mapped only the view from the end of the trail. A ring around the map traced the skyline in every direction. It showed a view that opens to the west.

One viewpoint cannot show when that view arrives. So the final map runs the viewshed along the whole walk and keeps the first point where each cell comes into view.

Inside the scripts

Three Python scripts build the map. day05_viewshed_check.py sets the viewpoint and runs the checks. day05_sight.py builds the terrain surface. day05_walk.py walks the trail and draws the still and the video.

The excerpts below are trimmed. A # ... line marks a cut.

The 1 m lidar covers a 1 km square around the viewpoint. The script averages it to 2 m cells. A weight then rises from 0 to 1 between 10 m and 30 m inside the tile edge. In that band, the surface is a mix of the two sources.

GDAL does the line-of-sight work. The script writes the surface once as a raw ENVI raster. Then it calls gdal_viewshed for each position. GDAL writes 1 for a visible cell and 0 for a hidden cell.

The -cc 0.85714 option lowers far ground for the curve of the earth and for light that bends in the air.

The walk loop is short. At each position, the eye height comes from the median lidar ground within 3 m along the line, so one bad cell does not move the eye. The last position is the viewpoint itself, set by the check script.

gdal_viewshed measures the observer height above its own surface. The script passes the eye height above the lidar ground, less the surface value under the observer. Each cell then keeps the first distance where it is visible. The red class needs only one comparison.

The script caches the 155 viewsheds under a hash of the viewpoint, the walk positions, the eye height and the GDAL settings. A change to the map style reuses the cache.

Checks

  • On the 10 m DEM, gdal_viewshed and a second, separate sightline sampler agree on more than 99.9% of cells within 12 km.
  • Against 6,000 exact sightlines to random cells, both methods are correct on 99.7% of them or more.
  • Within 475 m of the viewpoint, the 1 m lidar sees about 18% less ground than the 10 m DEM. That result is why the build uses lidar near the trail.
  • I took a phone video at the viewpoint. In three of its frames, the modeled skyline lies on the sky edge, with a median miss of about 0.1 degree.
  • A 10 m move in any direction at the viewpoint keeps 96% to 98% of the ground in view. The view belongs to the platform.

Symbol guide

  • Gold marks ground in view from some point on the trail.
  • Red marks ground that comes into view only at the rim, in the last 15 m.
  • In the video, gold is ground in view from the walker now. Pale gold is ground seen earlier on the walk.
  • Black dots mark summits on the skyline. Gray names with open circles mark peaks out of view.
  • The map is turned 20 degrees so Zion Canyon runs up the page. North points slightly left of up.
  • The strip under the map shows how much ground is in view at each point on the walk.
  • The labels follow the National Park Service map of Zion. Green labels mark visitor buildings, as on that map.

Data notes

  • The model is bare earth. Trees along the trail would block some glimpses.
  • The trail line is from the 2014 dataset, not a new survey. Near the rim, its position error moves the spot where the view opens by a few meters.
  • "The last 15 m" is the model's rim class. It is not a measured step.
  • The model has no haze, and it does not show how far a person can see on a given day.

Data and credit

The U.S. Geological Survey provides the 3DEP elevation data. The National Park Service provides the trail line and the park map that the labels follow. Peak names come from that map and from the USGS Geographic Names Information System (GNIS). Roads, creeks and rivers come from OpenStreetMap.
