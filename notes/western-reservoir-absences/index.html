The Reservoirs the Dashboard Does Not Show

151 of the West's 497 reservoirs of 10,000 acre-feet or more are not on the Western Water Dashboard. A map sorts them by kind of absence.

A sequel to Day 15

Day 15 counted 69 held back. Here they are, with 122 large reservoirs that have no finding yet.

The Western Water Dashboard shows how full the West's reservoirs are. A reservoir gets on its map only when a public source reports its storage and the record passes the dashboard's checks. This map looks at the other side: the large reservoirs that are not on the dashboard, and the reason for each.

151 of 497

The National Inventory of Dams lists 497 reservoirs of 10,000 acre-feet (12.3 million m³) or more in the dashboard's five water regions. The dashboard shows 346 of them. The other 151 fall into three kinds of absence:

  • Reviewed, held back: 29. The dashboard reviewed a record and wrote down why it does not show it. 23 are held back, 1 is retired and 1 is withdrawn. For 4 more, the finding is in the dashboard's own audit notes: Priest Lake, Crane Creek, Silver Lake in Montana and Olivenhain.
  • Level, not volume: 8. A source publishes the water level, but not the volume. Lake Washington, Lake Billy Chinook and C.J. Strike are examples. A volume needs a reviewed table that converts level to storage.
  • No reviewed storage series: 114. For 113, none of six sources that the dashboard already reads lists a storage series. The other is Pinecrest Lake: California's CDEC reports its storage every day, but the dashboard has no finding for it yet.

The 151 reservoirs hold about 10.9 million acre-feet in the dam inventory. The 497 hold about 179.4 million. These totals mix storage bases, so they show size, not a share of water.

Where the 69 held-back records are

Day 15's 69 count records, not reservoirs. A record is one station from one source. On this map:

  • 26 records sit on 23 large reservoirs. Success Lake has three records, and Milner Dam has two.
  • 34 records fall outside the 497, which count normal storage. 16 of them are flood-control basins in California, Arizona and Nevada. The basins are empty by design, so the inventory lists little or no normal storage for them. Others are small reservoirs, river stations, pools with no matching dam, or a second record for a lake. The map draws all 34 as small rings with no size.
  • 9 records measure water that the dashboard already shows through another record. Hells Canyon, Flathead Lake and Mann Creek are examples. The interactive map lists them.

How the marks work

Each kind of absence has one mark. The marks run from crisp to grainy as the certainty drops:

  • A gray dot is a reservoir on the dashboard.
  • A crisp hollow ring is a reviewed record with a written finding.
  • A ring with a bar is a level without a volume. The bar is a water line.
  • A grainy dotted ring is a reservoir with no reviewed storage series.

The choice follows two studies. In MacEachren and others (2012), readers rated fuzziness and lighter value as the most natural signs of uncertainty. Crisp edges read as certain. Afifah and Krisp (2026) propose grain as a texture layer for uncertainty. Ring size follows storage in the dam inventory.

Explore the map

Select any reservoir to see its status. For a reviewed record, the card quotes the dashboard's own finding. Search by the name of a dam or lake, or list the reservoirs off the map by kind. Open the full map.

What "no reviewed storage series" means

It means that no storage series turned up where the search looked. It does not mean that no number exists.

  • The search read the storage catalogs of USGS, California CDEC, the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, Reclamation, NRCS and Colorado DWR on November 10, 2026.
  • Level series and operator websites were searched for only 13 large reservoirs. That search found the eight level-only reservoirs.
  • In the dam inventory, irrigation is the main purpose of 45 of the 113. Water supply is the main purpose of 23, and hydropower of 12.

Inside the scripts

Four Python scripts build the map from the dashboard's own files at one commit. They read the files with git show, so every count comes from code, not from a note. The scripts pass the default checks of the ruff linter and use its code format. The excerpt below is trimmed. A # ... line marks a cut.

Each reservoir takes its kind from the first rule that applies. The dashboard's own status comes first, then a finding in the dashboard's own audit notes. A short table of hand searches, each with its source, comes next. Everything left has no reviewed storage series: a current feed from the catalog check with no finding, or no series found at all.
