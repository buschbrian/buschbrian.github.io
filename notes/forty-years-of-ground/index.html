30 Day Map Challenge 2026 | Day 20: Hexagons

Forty Years of Ground

Utah's land cover in 1,023 hexagons, 10 miles across. Each hexagon shows its mix of land cover and stands at its mean elevation. Six hexagons show how the land changed from 1985 to 2025.

ChallengeDay 20: Hexagons
SubjectUtah land cover, 1985 to 2025
Made withBlender 5.2 (Cycles), Python (Rasterio, NumPy, GeoPandas, Shapely, pyproj, Matplotlib)
Challenge dateNovember 20, 2026

Utah is mostly shrub

Shrub covered 62 percent of Utah in 1985. It covered 62 percent in 2025 too. Most of the state did not change.

The change is at the edges, where people live. Developed land grew from 1,139 to 1,834 square miles (2,950 to 4,750 km²). That is about 61 percent more. About two-fifths of the new developed land is in Salt Lake, Utah, Davis and Weber counties.

The two bars at the left of the poster show the mix for the whole state:

  • Shrub and scrub: 62.4 percent in 1985, 62.0 percent in 2025
  • Forest: 19.9 to 19.4 percent
  • Barren: 6.4 to 7.7 percent
  • Grassland: 2.0 to 3.1 percent
  • Water and wetland: 4.6 to 2.9 percent
  • Crops and pasture: 3.4 to 2.6 percent
  • Developed: 1.3 to 2.2 percent

How to read a hexagon

Each hexagon is 10 miles (16 km) across. Its top is a mosaic of 24 triangles. The rarest land cover in the hexagon takes the triangles at the center. The most common land cover takes the triangles on the rim. So a hexagon that is mostly forest has a green rim.

Each triangle is about 4 percent of the hexagon. A class with a smaller share can fall between two triangles and not show. The shares in the text and the charts come from every 30 m cell of land cover, not from the triangles.

The height of a hexagon is its mean elevation, from 2,805 to 11,489 ft (855 to 3,502 m). The poster raises each hexagon 8 times its height above 2,000 ft (610 m). The high plateaus and the Uinta Mountains stand up, and the forest is on top of them.

Six places that changed

Each panel shows one hexagon from above at true scale: an aerial photo, the land cover in 1985 and in 2025, and a line for every year. I chose the hexagons that had the largest gain or loss of one class from 1985 to 2025, plus the most developed hexagon in the state and one from southern Utah.

  • Great Salt Lake, north arm, Box Elder County. Water fell from 98 to 5 percent, and barren land rose from 1 to 94 percent. Most of the drop came before 2005.
  • Box Elder County, at Blue Creek and Howell. Crops and pasture fell from 63 to 26 percent. Grassland rose from 6 to 28 percent.
  • American Fork and Lehi, Utah County. Developed land rose from 23 to 65 percent. Crops and pasture fell from 35 to 4 percent.
  • West Jordan and South Jordan, Salt Lake County. Developed land rose from 56 to 87 percent. This is the most developed hexagon in Utah. West Jordan has 51 percent of its new developed land, and South Jordan has 26 percent.
  • Payson Lakes, Utah County, in the Uinta-Wasatch-Cache National Forest. Forest fell from 71 to 20 percent. It was 70 percent in 2017, 42 percent in 2018 and 20 percent in 2019.
  • St. George and Washington, Washington County. Developed land rose from 11 to 33 percent.

Two of these need context. The Great Salt Lake hexagon starts in 1985, just before the lake's record high in 1986 and 1987. Its drop starts near a peak.

Two fires burned the Payson Lakes hexagon in 2018. The Pole Creek fire burned 101,875 acres (41,230 ha), and its perimeter covers 55 percent of the hexagon. The Bald Mountain fire burned 18,603 acres (7,530 ha), and its perimeter covers 26 percent. The change from forest to shrub is the land cover after the fires.

Projection, scale and area

The hexagons use an Albers equal-area projection for Utah, with standard parallels at 37.5° and 41.5° north and a central meridian at 111.5° west. The land cover is counted on its own 30 m grid, which is also equal-area. The land cover is not resampled.

I checked the areas. Utah is 219,881 km² (84,897 square miles) in the projection and 219,882 km² on the ellipsoid. Each hexagon agrees with its area on the ellipsoid within 0.04 percent.

The map is seen from the south, tilted 40° from straight down, with no perspective. All of the tops shrink by the same amount, so the areas on the map keep their ratios. Distances from north to south on the map are 0.77 times the distances from east to west. The scale bar on the map points north and measures distance from north to south. The panels are drawn straight down at true scale.

A tall hexagon can hide part of the hexagon behind it. I measured this against a flat render from the same camera. The hexagons hide 0.8 percent of the top area.

The colors

Each color is one class of land cover. Shrub covers most of the state, so it has the quietest color. Developed land, water and crops are small but important, so they have the strongest colors. I tested the colors with simulations of the three common types of color blindness.

Inside the scripts

Python scripts prepare the data and make the poster.

  • day20_fetch.py gets the land cover for Utah from the USGS map service.
  • day20_grid.py makes the hexagons and counts the land cover in each one.
  • day20_glyphs.py makes the mosaic of 24 triangles.
  • day20_terrace_prep.py gives each hexagon its mean elevation.
  • day20_blender_terrace.py renders the hexagons in Blender.
  • day20_panels_data.py gets every year of land cover and the aerial photos for the six hexagons.
  • day20_poster.py makes the poster.

Method

  • Land cover is Annual NLCD Collection 1.2 from USGS EROS, at 30 m, for every year from 1985 to 2025. The 16 classes go into seven groups.
  • The hexagons are 10 miles (16 km) across, from flat side to flat side. Hexagons on the state line are cut to Utah. A small piece of a hexagon on the border joins the hexagon next to it.
  • The mean elevation of each hexagon comes from USGS 3DEP at about 1 km.
  • The map is three renders from one camera in Blender. One gives the exact colors, one gives the light and shadow, and one separates the tops from the sides. The tops keep their colors, with only a light shadow.
  • The aerial photos are USDA NAIP from 2021. Each panel gives the months of its photos.
  • The place names come from USGS GNIS. Each name on a photo is inside its hexagon.
  • The county and city shares use boundaries from the Utah Geospatial Resource Center. A hexagon belongs to the county under its center.
  • The poster is 2160 by 2700 pixels. The page shows the same image.

Limits

  • NLCD classifies land from satellite images. Some cells have the wrong class, and the class of a cell can change from year to year without a change on the ground.
  • Developed land in NLCD includes developed open space, such as parks, lawns and the land next to roads. It is not only buildings and pavement.
  • A mosaic of 24 triangles rounds each share. In a full hexagon the error is up to 4 percent. In a hexagon on the state line, which has less land, it is up to 11.5 percent.
  • The aerial photos are from 2021. The land cover is from 2025.
  • The Great Salt Lake hexagon starts near the lake's highest level. A different start year gives a smaller change.

Data and credit

USGS EROS provides the Annual NLCD land cover. The U.S. Geological Survey 3D Elevation Program provides the elevation. The aerial photos are USDA NAIP through The National Map. The National Interagency Fire Center provides the fire perimeters. The Utah Geospatial Resource Center, the USDA Forest Service and the U.S. Census Bureau provide the boundaries. The place names come from the USGS Geographic Names Information System.
