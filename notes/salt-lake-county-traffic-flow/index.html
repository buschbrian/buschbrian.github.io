Traffic Flow on Salt Lake County's State Highways

A print map of annual average daily traffic in 2025. The width of each line shows the traffic on one count section of the state highway system.

OccasionGIS Day
Subject2025 traffic on state highways in Salt Lake County, Utah
Made withPython (GeoPandas, Shapely, rasterio, NumPy, pandas, Matplotlib, Pillow, PyMuPDF), Adobe Illustrator, FFmpeg
PublishedNovember 18, 2026

A map from the Esri User Conference

GIS Day is on November 18 this year. GIS means geographic information system. For GIS Day, I share a traffic map of Salt Lake County, Utah.

At the 2025 Esri User Conference, I saw a traffic flow map of San Diego County by Caltrans, the California Department of Transportation. The map uses one color. The width of each line shows how much traffic the road carries, and a small number beside the line gives the count.

I wanted the same kind of map for Salt Lake County, in a desert color. I made a first version in ArcGIS Pro and planned to finish it in Adobe Illustrator.

What the map shows

Annual average daily traffic (AADT) is the traffic on a road section for one year, divided by 365. The values come from Utah's 2025 submission to the Highway Performance Monitoring System (HPMS) of the Federal Highway Administration. I rounded each value to the nearest hundred.

  • The busiest section is I-15 at the I-80 interchange in South Salt Lake. It carries 342,800 vehicles a day.
  • The map has 430 count sections. 346 of them have a label. The other 84 did not have room.
  • Ramps are thin lines without a volume. The faint lines are other arterial roads.

The gaps at the ramps

The first version had gaps at the interchanges. Its traffic layers had no ramps, or ramps in one direction only. The lines stopped where two highways meet. This is a common problem on traffic flow maps.

The 2025 HPMS data has the ramps in both directions. At the interchanges that I checked, the highways now continue without a break. The ramps connect each route to the freeway.

Six line widths

The San Diego map uses four line widths. Its first class is all roads under 100,000 vehicles a day. About 85 percent of the state highway miles on this map are in that class. With four widths, most of the network would be one line weight.

This map uses six widths, with breaks at 10,000, 25,000, 50,000, 100,000 and 200,000. The valley arterials now look different from the canyon roads.

From Python to Illustrator

I did not finish the map in Illustrator as I planned. I built the full sheet in Python. Python placed the labels, the route markers and the place names. It also made the shore of Great Salt Lake at 4,205 feet from a USGS elevation model of the lakebed.

The route markers are the official public domain artwork on Wikimedia Commons. Commons stopped sending files to my network, so I made 25 of the Utah markers from the digits of the official ones.

At the end, Python wrote an Illustrator file with named layers, text that you can edit, and character styles. I will finish the print in both Python and Illustrator.

Data and credit

  • Traffic: Federal Highway Administration, Highway Performance Monitoring System, Utah, data year 2025. The Utah Department of Transportation (UDOT) submits these data.
  • Shaded relief: USGS 3D Elevation Program, 5 m.
  • Great Salt Lake: the USGS topobathymetric model at 4,205 feet, with causeways and dikes from OpenStreetMap contributors.
  • Utah Lake, roads, counties and city boundaries: the Utah Geospatial Resource Center (UGRC). Utah Lake comes from the USGS National Hydrography Dataset through UGRC.
  • Route markers: Wikimedia Commons, public domain.
