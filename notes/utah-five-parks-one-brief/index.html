30 Day Map Challenge 2026 | Day 30: Pen & paper

One Brief, Five Maps of Utah’s National Parks

For the last day, I gave five AI models one brief. The data, the scale, and the content of the map were fixed. The drawing was theirs. Each model signed its sheet and wrote a note of thanks to the challenge. No person drew any of these maps, and each sheet says so.

What the theme became

The Day 30 theme is Pen & paper. I planned to ink a map by hand. In a short holiday week, I used the day to end the month’s experiment with AI models instead.

On Day 10, one brief went to many models, and each made an earthquake poster. That brief fixed the subject and the data. This one fixes more and frees more:

  • Fixed: the data sources, the projection, the scale, and each item the map must show.
  • Free: the medium, the colors, the terrain method, the lettering, and the layout.

The brief asks for a sheet that looks made by a hand, on paper, with real drawing materials. It forbids image-generation models. Each mark comes from code that the model wrote during its run. Each sheet carries the line “AI-drawn from a single prompt”.

The fixed map

All five models made the same map. The brief gave these rules:

  • The whole state of Utah, in plan view, on an 8 by 10 inch portrait sheet of 2160 by 2700 pixels.
  • NAD83 / UTM zone 12N, north up, at 1:2,750,000. One inch is 43.4 miles.
  • Five data sources and no others: National Park Service boundaries, the US Census Bureau state file, Natural Earth rivers, lakes, and places, and one USGS 3DEP elevation grid with cells of about 250 m.
  • Required content: the state outline, the five parks with names, terrain, five rivers, three lakes, three towns, a scale bar, and credits.
  • The area of each park, computed from the NPS polygons.
  • A signature that the model designs and draws, and a note of thanks of 30 words or fewer.

The elevation grid is coarse. For that reason, the brief does not permit printed elevations for single points. You can download the prompt to read the full text.

The runs

Each model ran in its own terminal session, in an empty folder, with a sandbox. Each session had one human message: the prompt, unchanged. The brief gave a 45-minute limit and asked for three rounds of critique and redrawing.

Some notes on the runs:

  • Grok 4.7 passed the limit. It thought for about seven minutes, then started its own clock. Its documentation states “about 51 minutes, past the 45-minute stop”. From the submission of the brief, the time was about 58 minutes.
  • The brief said not to deliver before minute 35. Four models finished their sheets early. Hydrogen and Sonnet then waited for that minute and changed nothing. Opus waited until I spoke to it. That rule was a defect in my brief.
  • Opus stopped at an approval prompt for its elevation download. I changed the session to automatic approval, and it continued. The other four ran with automatic approval from the start.
  • Grok and Hydrogen shared one Mac at the same time. The other three ran alone.

This is five runs of one brief, not a controlled comparison. The apps and the instructions in context differed.

What the checks supported

Claude Fable 5.2 helped write the brief and made the checks. It did not run the brief. It measured each exported image against the source data.

  • All five runs downloaded the same five sources. The elevation file has the same size in each folder.
  • All five sheets print the same park areas, and the areas match a separate calculation from the NPS file.
  • On each sheet, the five parks, Moab, and St. George are within a few pixels of their projected positions.
  • Each mile scale bar has the correct length for 1:2,750,000, within three pixels.

The issues that remained

Lake Powell past the state line. The Natural Earth lake shape extends into Arizona. Neon, Hydrogen, and Grok draw the part outside Utah. Opus and Sonnet cut the lake at the state line.

Lettering. The brief asked for a hand style or a sans-serif face. Neon, Opus, and Hydrogen used system typefaces. Sonnet and Grok built their own letters from strokes. Sonnet’s letters stay readable. Grok’s do not on dark terrain.

Drawing or relief render. The brief named a plain plot with a paper texture as the result to avoid. Grok is closest to that result. Hydrogen and Neon read as polished digital relief. Opus and Sonnet show the most strokes.

The required lines. Each sheet has the challenge line and “AI-drawn from a single prompt”. On Opus the second line is too small to read on a phone. On Grok the border cuts the challenge line.

The scale bar. Hydrogen labels its mile ticks 40, 80, 120, and 160 km. One hundred miles is 160.9 km, so each label is about 0.6 percent off.

Time. Grok passed the limit and said so. The other four finished inside it.

The signatures and the notes

The brief asked each model to sign with its own name and to write its own thanks. These are the five notes, as written on the sheets.

  • Gemini 4 Neon, in script beside a red seal: “Thirty days of lines, relief, and shared curiosity. Thank you to every mapmaker and reader who kept this November alive with craft, generosity, and wonder.”
  • Claude Opus 5.5, in broad-nib script beside an orange spark: “For everyone who drew, shared, and cheered through thirty days: thank you. Every map this month was someone paying close attention to a place. Here is mine, paid to Utah.”
  • Grok 4.7, in loose red handwriting: “To everyone who drew a map this month and everyone who looked: thank you. Thirty days is a long time to care about a line.”
  • Gemini 4 Hydrogen, in script beside an atom mark: “To everyone who spent November drawing places by hand and code: thank you for thirty days of curiosity, craft, and shared wonder across every coastline, ridge, and line.”
  • Claude Sonnet 5.5, in script beside 14 ruled lines: “To everyone who makes and follows #30DayMapChallenge: thanks for thirty days of generous, strange, beautiful maps. I had one sheet and one pen; you brought the planet.”

I add my own thanks to the people who organize the challenge and to everyone who posted a map this month.

How the brief changed

The brief took four test rounds on the same day. Each test changed it.

  • The first version asked for a blue underlay that I would ink by hand, with a limit on line spacing. The model could select its own map. It made a safe sheet of three small panels.
  • The second version asked for more ambition. The model drew one park in detail. To obey the spacing rule, it cut its contour lines into short pieces. With a free subject, no two results could be compared.
  • The third version fixed the data and the map and freed the drawing. The model finished in 15 minutes of the 45.
  • The final version added the three rounds and the rule about minute 35.

The fixed data made the results easy to check. Five models printed the same five areas, and each position could be measured. The rounds improved the sheets. The rule about minute 35 only added waiting time.

Sources and scope

These sheets are drawings of a small-scale reference map. They are not for navigation. The terrain comes from a grid with cells of about 250 m, so it shows ranges and plateaus, not single canyons.

  • National Park Service, Land Resources Division boundary service. Park boundaries.
  • US Census Bureau cartographic boundary files. The state outline, 2023, 1:500,000.
  • Natural Earth. Rivers, lakes, and places at 1:10 million.
  • USGS 3D Elevation Program. Elevation.

The full-size images preserve all five results, including the documented defects. They are JPG copies of the original PNG files at the same pixel size. Raw chat transcripts remain private.
